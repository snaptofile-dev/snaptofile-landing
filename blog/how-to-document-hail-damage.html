How to Document Hail Damage for an Insurance Claim

The difference between a paid claim and a denied one often comes down to documentation. A step-by-step field guide for independent adjusters and roofing contractors.

A
Albert Miller
Former Independent Adjuster · Founder, SnapToFile

Why Documentation Makes or Breaks a Hail Claim

Carriers don't approve claims based on what happened. They approve them based on what you can prove happened. A roof with legitimate hail damage gets denied every day because the field documentation was incomplete, disorganized, or unprofessional.

Good documentation does three things:

  • Establishes that hail actually fell at that specific location on a specific date
  • Connects the hail event to the observed damage, not wear, not age, not installation defect
  • Quantifies the damage well enough that an estimator can scope it without guessing

If your report can do all three, approval becomes straightforward.

Step 1: Verify the Storm Event Before You Arrive

Before you set foot on the property, confirm the storm data. This protects you and anchors your report.

  • Pull a hail verification report from a service like CoreLogic, Verisk Weather, or HailTrace for the property address and date of loss
  • Note the reported hail size. Quarter-inch is nuisance, one-inch and above starts causing functional damage to most roofing materials
  • Screenshot or save the report; it goes in your documentation package as exhibit one

If the storm data doesn't show hail at that address on that date, document what you find and write it up honestly.

Step 2: Gear Up Before You Climb

You don't need expensive equipment. You need the right equipment ready before the inspection starts.

What to bring:

  • Your phone (camera) fully charged
  • A measuring tape or hail size reference card
  • A soapstone or sidewalk chalk for marking test squares
  • A notepad or your documentation app open and ready
  • A tape measure for ridge and eave lengths

Optional camera settings to check:

  • Location/GPS tagging turned ON, which embeds the property coordinates in every photo
  • Date and time confirmed accurate, as timestamps in metadata matter for date-of-loss disputes
  • HDR, auto-enhance, and live photo mode turned OFF, since you want natural light photos, not processed ones

Step 3: Establish the Property Before You Document Damage

Every good report starts with context photos. These prove you were at the right property and give the reviewer a frame of reference before they look at damage.

Take these shots first:

  1. Street-level establishing shot, full front of the property with the address visible if possible
  2. All four elevations, standing back far enough to capture the full roof slope: front, rear, left side, right side
  3. Overhead or drone shot if available, showing the full roof footprint at once
  4. Nearby reference objects, like neighboring roofs, fences, metal outbuildings. These become supporting evidence later.

Label these clearly. "Front Elevation" or "Front Slope." To give even more clarity, note the cardinal direction: "Front faces south" in the body or description area.

Field Tip

Slope refers to the roof facets. Elevation refers to the sided facets of the home. Keeping these terms consistent in your reports builds credibility with reviewers.

Step 4: Document the Supporting Evidence First

Here's what a lot of new adjusters get backwards: they head straight for the roof. Start at ground level instead. The soft metals and secondary surfaces tell the story before you ever climb a ladder.

Hail hits on gutters, downspouts, AC condenser fins, window screens, and painted wood are almost impossible to fake or confuse with other damage. They corroborate what you find on the roof.

Work your way around the property and photograph:

  • Gutters and downspouts, looking for circular impact dimples, fresh dents with exposed bare metal. Photograph each elevation's gutters separately.
  • AC condenser unit; the aluminum fins dent easily. This is one of the best indicators of hail size and density. Get close, get the light right.
  • Window screens; punctures or tears in screen mesh are clear impact evidence
  • Painted wood surfaces, including fascia boards, window sills, wood trim. Fresh impacts show raw wood or chipped paint with no weathering
  • Vehicles parked on property at time of loss; document dents if the owner allows. Note clearly that vehicle documentation is supplementary only.
  • Soft metal roof penetrations, including plumbing vents, pipe boots, lead flashings. These show hits even when shingles are debatable.

For each item: photograph the full object first (context), then move in tight on individual impacts. Both shots matter.

Field Tip

Starting a routine like always beginning at the front and moving right sets up a reliable pattern. When you're stressed for time or overwhelmed on a large loss, you automatically revert to: front, right, rear, left, overviews first, close-ups second. Before you know it the documentation is done and in order. On really large properties, break your photos into folders by elevation or trade.

Step 5: Document the Roof Surface Systematically

Now you climb. Work one slope at a time, in a consistent pattern. Don't jump around.

What you're looking for on asphalt shingles

Functional hail damage (what carriers care about) looks like:

  • Bruising, a soft spot in the mat beneath the granules, often with a dark center where granules were knocked away. Press with your thumb; a true bruise has little to no spring-back.
  • Granule displacement; fresh impacts show bare fiberglass mat. The missing granules will be in the gutters below.
  • Fractured or cracked mat; on older or more brittle shingles, direct hits crack the fiberglass mat itself
  • Spatter marks, the mud/debris impact ring around a hit, visible in low-angle light

What is NOT functional hail damage

  • Granule loss from foot traffic or age
  • Blistering, a manufacturing defect that looks like impacts to the untrained eye
  • Cracking from thermal cycling
  • Previous repairs

How to photograph roof hits

  1. Chalk a test square, marking a 10x10 foot area and count hits within it. Multiple test squares across the roof give you a hit density count, which matters for functional damage thresholds.
  2. Low-angle shots; crouch down and shoot at a low angle across the shingle surface. Use a folder, notepad, or other large object to create full shade for your entire photo. This reveals bruising and spatter marks that disappear in straight-down shots.
  3. Close-up with scale reference; get a tape measure or coin next to individual impacts so the size is documentable
  4. Straight-down overview of the test square, showing hit pattern and density together

Repeat this for every slope. Don't shortcut the rear slope because it's inconvenient; carriers notice missing slopes.

On metal roofing

Hail hits on metal leave clean circular dents. Photograph with raking light (low angle, side-lit) to reveal the shadow of each dent. Count and document hit density the same way.

On tile roofing

Document cracked, fractured, or displaced tiles. Photograph whole tiles from above, then close-ups of individual cracks. Note whether the crack pattern is consistent with impact (star or radial fracture) versus foot traffic (straight-line fracture).

Step 6: Photograph the Interior If There's a Water Intrusion Claim

If the homeowner reports interior damage:

  • Document the stain or damage in context (the whole room or ceiling area)
  • Close-up of the stain or affected material
  • Photograph directly above on the roof and note the corresponding area
  • Note whether the interior damage is consistent with the roof damage location

Don't speculate on causation in photos. Let the images and your written notes do that work.

Step 7: Organize Everything Before You Leave the Property

The biggest mistake in field documentation is leaving with a camera roll full of unlabeled photos in random order. Organization happens on-site, not later at your desk.

For each section of the report, group photos as:

  1. Context/establishing shot
  2. Mid-range, showing the damaged component in relation to surrounding area
  3. Close-up with damage clearly visible

Minimum Photo Checklist: Before You Leave

  • Address/property establishing shot
  • All four elevations
  • Gutters, all elevations
  • AC condenser
  • At least two soft metal surfaces
  • Chalk square test with count, each slope
  • Low-angle damage shots, each slope
  • Close-ups with scale reference, each slope
  • Any interior damage (if applicable)

If you're missing anything on this list, get it before you come down the ladder.

Step 8: Write the Report While It's Fresh

Field notes written same-day are dramatically better than ones written three days later. You'll remember details like the hit count in the test square on the north slope and the dent pattern on the condenser, details that evaporate quickly.

Your written report should include:

  • Date of inspection and date of loss
  • Storm verification summary (hail size, source)
  • Property description (roof type, approximate age, pitch, square footage)
  • Supporting evidence summary: what you found on gutters, AC, soft metals
  • Slope-by-slope damage summary with hit counts, damage types observed, and your conclusion on functional damage
  • Photo reference; photos should appear adjacent to the relevant section, not dumped at the end

Common Mistakes That Get Claims Denied or Disputed

  • Missing elevation: one slope or elevation with no photos reads as "nothing to document"
  • No supporting evidence; roof photos alone are easier to dispute
  • No scale reference; impacts without size context are harder to evaluate
  • Blistering misidentified as hail, a common error that damages your credibility with reviewers who catch it
  • Disorganized photo order; a 200-photo dump with no structure makes reviewers work harder and like you less

Summary

Good hail damage documentation follows a clear sequence: verify the storm, establish the property, document soft metals and supporting evidence, work the roof systematically with test squares and close-ups, and write your notes while they're fresh.

A complete, organized report with strong photo evidence is the difference between a smooth claim approval and an uphill dispute. The goal isn't just to document damage; it's to tell a clear, credible story that any reviewer can follow without having been there.
